Analytical Context

Lanchester's square law (1916) formalizes why concentration of force matters under aimed fire: a unit's combat power scales with the square of its numbers times the per-unit effectiveness. Engel's 1954 study used Iwo Jima casualty data as one of the few empirical "validations." Policy Tensor invokes it to needle purely attritional, materialist models — because if outcomes were just numbers and firepower, you couldn't explain why the eventual winner often emerges stronger after absorbing heavy losses.

His resolution is the "war-making as skilled work" thesis: extended wars are learning systems. Each side iterates on tactics, organization, and technology faster than attrition degrades it. Jarabik's Ukraine observation — that "systems matter more than platforms" (recon-strike networks, AI-assisted targeting, Starlink) and that both sides adapt — is the contemporary instance. The Quester quote supplies the rationalist counterpoint: if war is so legible, why do both sides routinely expect to win? Mutual optimism (a core puzzle in bargaining theory) is exactly what skilled-work and learning dynamics make possible.

The Kettering Bug (a 1918 aerial torpedo) and the Dark Eagle hypersonic note bookend the argument in hardware: today's "drone revolution" has a century-old lineage, and long-range precision systems reshape deterrence calculations far from their intended theater — flexibility that "could pose issues for Moscow."
